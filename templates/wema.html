Two ways into this list. Most stocks qualify on average turnover clearing the floor — liquidity is a property of the stock, so a single session's figure made membership arbitrary. But a second route admits stocks whose average sits below the floor while they trade three times or more their own norm, provided that expanded turnover still clears the floor in absolute terms. Those rows carry an amber multiple beside the turnover. Excluding them was backwards: a stock running four times its usual volume into a 200-week level is more interesting than a large-cap drifting past one, and a thin stock at five times nothing is still filtered out.

Why so few results, and what the floor costs you. Only a small share of any universe sits at a 200-week EMA in a given week — that is what makes the level meaningful, so a couple of dozen hits from a few hundred names is the expected yield. But the floor is applied to a single session's turnover, which is noisy: a stock whose normal turnover is ₹20 Cr will pass on a high-volume week and fail on a quiet one. If a name you expected is missing, check its average turnover before assuming the scan is wrong — and widen the floor if its normal level sits below it.

What each column means. Dist is how far this week's close sits from the EMA. Pierce is how deep the week's low went below it (for support tests) or how far the high went above it (for rejections) — a deep pierce that still closed the right side is a stronger defence than a shallow tag. Wick is the rejection wick as a share of the week's range. Vol× compares this week's volume to the 20-week average; below 1.0 means the level was tested without conviction.

Score weights confirmation, not attractiveness: volume expansion, pierce depth, wick geometry, and EMA slope agreement. It deliberately penalises levels that have been crossed three or more times recently, since a level price keeps cutting through is not being respected.

History column. A 200-period EMA needs far more than 200 bars to settle — after only 85 bars past the seed, roughly 43% of the value is still the seed rather than real price action, which is why short history produces numbers well off a full-history chart. This scan now pulls up to 16 years per stock. An amber ⚠ marks any stock where more than 10% of its EMA is still seed influence; treat those values as approximate and check the chart directly.

The honest caveat. Thousands of stocks touch a long moving average in any given week — a touch alone is not a signal, it is a location. This scan tells you where price is relative to a widely-watched level and how it behaved on arrival. Whether that resolves up or down depends on things not in this data.
