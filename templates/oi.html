F&O POSITIONING · OPEN INTEREST—

What positions are being built

Futures buildup crossed with option flow, to show whether futures exposure is being hedged with options or run naked.

What this can and cannot show. Open interest counts contracts outstanding, and every contract has a buyer and a seller — so OI alone never reveals who initiated a position or who is on which side. Combining OI change with price change permits an inference about who was aggressive, which is what this page does. It is a well-established inference, not a measurement.

Raw numbers are normalised against each contract's own history. A 4% futures OI move is seven standard deviations in a contract that rarely shifts, and well within normal for one that swings daily — so OI change is scored in deviations, not percent. The same applies to PCR: 0.82 is the top of the range for a stock that normally sits at 0.6 and the bottom for one that sits at 1.3, so it is reported as a percentile of that stock's own range rather than against the usual rules of thumb, which were built for indices. Option OI direction is tracked across sessions for the same reason a single day of delivery means little.

The expiry signal weighs the strands and reports disagreement as disagreement. Futures buildup carries double weight, more if the OI move is statistically unusual; call and put flow, PCR position and multi-day option OI each add one. When the strands point opposite ways the result is Mixed rather than a forced call — one side positioning against the other is itself information, and flattening it into a direction would discard the most useful part.

Bank Nifty is included. It settles monthly, so its expiry cycle matches the stock series and the comparison holds. Nifty and the other indices are excluded because their weekly expiries make "nearest expiry" mean something different — the OI numbers would not be comparable with a monthly stock contract.

Which expiry this covers. Everything here — futures OI, option OI, PCR, max pain, the 5-day trend — is the nearest expiry only. Later months are excluded, which matters as expiry approaches: positions roll forward, current-month OI falls, and that looks identical to unwinding. Next-month OI is now tracked alongside so rollover can be identified and flagged rather than misread as conviction leaving.

What delivery does and does not capture. It correctly excludes intraday trading — positions squared off the same day net to zero and never reach settlement. It does not exclude MTF purchases (margin-funded, delivered into the broker's account against a loan), BTST positions, or the cash leg of cash-and-carry arbitrage, all of which take delivery and look identical to outright buying. So high delivery removes the noisiest category of activity, but it is not proof of unleveraged conviction. Note the irony on the arbitrage point: high delivery alongside a futures short buildup may be the arbitrage cash leg, which is why that combination is flagged separately rather than read as accumulation.

How delivery is judged. Not against a fixed threshold — some counters routinely settle 20% of volume into demat and others 70%, so a blanket cutoff flags the same names every day and misses a genuine surge in a habitually low-delivery stock. Each figure is compared to that stock's own average over the last 15 sessions: measured in standard deviations from that stock's own mean, not as a fixed multiple. A ratio threshold cannot work: 1.15× its average is nearly two deviations for a stock whose delivery sits in a tight band, and half a deviation for one that swings between 20% and 80%. Beyond +2 sd is treated as significant, +1 to +2 as raised, below −1 as weak — which matches how published delivery screens normalise the figure. Where a run of consecutive above-average sessions exists it is shown too, since a single high day can be a block deal or an index rebalance while a three-to-five session run is the accumulation footprint practitioners actually watch. Delivery is checked before basis when classifying a buildup, because real stock changing hands is stronger evidence than the carry.

Why cash-market data is here. Futures OI alone cannot tell genuine bearish speculation apart from cash-and-carry arbitrage — buying stock in cash and selling futures against it produces identical short OI with no directional view whatsoever. Two things separate them: the basis, since arbitrage only pays when futures are rich to spot, and delivery percentage, since an arbitrageur takes delivery while a day trader does not. Both are now folded into the verdict on each card, and stocks where short OI is probably arbitrage are flagged in amber rather than reported as bearish.

On Sensibull and similar platforms. They have no public API, and there would be no advantage anyway — they consume the same NSE exchange feed. Where NSE helps is that its option chain endpoint publishes changeinOpenInterest directly, which is the exchange's own day-over-day delta. This page uses that when NSE responds, which is what makes same-day analysis possible.

On "smart money". The only genuine participant attribution is NSE's daily participant-wise OI file, splitting FII, DII, Pro and Client. That is end-of-day and not available through a broker API. Nothing here identifies who is trading — it identifies what structure is being built.

Futures classification. Price up with OI up is a long buildup — new money committing on the long side. Price down with OI up is a short buildup. Price up with OI down is short covering, and price down with OI down is long unwinding. Covering and unwinding are weaker signals: old positions leaving, not new conviction arriving.

Option flow. Rising OI with a falling premium suggests writers came out ahead on price; rising OI with a rising premium suggests buyers did. Premium also moves with the underlying and with implied volatility, so the underlying's expected effect is stripped out first — that removes the largest source of false reads, not all of them.

This says who won on price, not who was crossing the spread. Passive buyers absorbing aggressive sellers produce rising OI and a rising premium, and look identical here to aggressive buying. Only tick-level order flow tells those apart.

Why the combination matters. A futures long paired with call writing is a capped, income-seeking position. The same futures long paired with put buying is a conviction position paying for insurance. Identical futures data, opposite messages about confidence — and both invisible if you look at futures or options alone.

Single day vs the run. One day's OI change is a snapshot and can mislead in both directions: a stock five sessions into steady accumulation may post its smallest increment today and look quiet, while a one-day spike on a flat base looks identical to real conviction. Each card therefore also shows the 5-day futures OI trend, a conviction score weighting size by how one-directional the build has been, and whether today confirms, diverges from or merely pauses that run. The bars are daily OI changes across the window.

Max pain is the strike where writers' total payout is smallest. The idea that price gravitates toward it is contested and the level shifts as OI moves, so treat it as context, never a target.
