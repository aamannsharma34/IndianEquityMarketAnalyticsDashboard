Analysing price structure…

HIGH VOL — a high-volume node: heavy positions changed hands in this range, so participants defend it. Acts as a wall.   THIN — a low-volume node: prices the market rejected. Acts as a vacuum, price travels through fast.   FVG — an unfilled imbalance from a fast move.   BUY-SIDE / SELL-SIDE — untested swing levels where stops cluster; multiple equal levels mean stacked stops.   An amber left border marks confluence — two or more independent zone types landing on the same price.

Reach rate is measured, not predicted: across every past window of this length on this stock, the share that saw price travel at least this far in this direction. It reflects distance and volatility — nothing about the zone itself.   Strength counts what's waiting at that price: gap size, stacked stops, volume, touch count, recency, confluence. The chips list exactly what earned it.

Read these two numbers separately. A near zone will always show a high reach rate simply because small moves are common — that is not evidence the zone matters. A distant zone with 90 strength and a 15% reach rate is genuinely significant but unlikely to be touched soon. The useful cases are where both are high. And a reach rate is not a probability that the zone will hold or reverse price — only that price is likely to get there. Nothing here accounts for news, earnings, or market regime, and the base rate is drawn from a period that may look nothing like the one ahead.
Volume-weighted support and resistance. Levels are built from confirmed swing pivots — a pivot is only used once enough later bars exist to prove it was a turning point, so nothing appears in hindsight and levels never move retrospectively.

Strength comes from the volume behind a level, not from how often a line was touched. Every re-test adds its volume to the zone, and nearby pivots merge into it rather than creating competing levels, so a zone defended repeatedly reads stronger without any touch-count rule doing the work. The centre is a volume-weighted average, so it drifts toward where volume actually sits.

Widths are set by ATR, so a zone on a volatile instrument is proportionally wider than one on a quiet one. Price rarely turns at an exact number, and a fixed band would be too tight on one stock and meaningless on another.

A zone can contain the current price. When it does it is marked PRICE INSIDE and sits at the price line rather than above or below it. The role label still reads as it was — a zone born from a swing high stays resistance until price closes decisively beyond it, and trading within the band is not a break. The level it must close through to flip is shown on the row.

Wicks strengthen, closes invalidate. A wick through a zone that closes back inside is evidence the level held under pressure and adds to its strength. Only a decisive close beyond counts as a break — and a break flips the role rather than deleting the level. Support closed decisively below becomes resistance. A second break after flipping retires the zone, since the market has stopped respecting it. FLIPPED* means inverted once and not broken since — nothing more. Touches are not tracked separately before vs after the flip, so the tag cannot tell you the level has been defended in its new role; the defended count on a flipped zone mixes both eras.

Computing execution metrics…

ADV — average daily traded value. The basic capacity number.   Tradeable size — 10% of ADV, a common single-day ceiling before impact turns material.   Amihud — basis points of price movement per ₹1 crore traded. Lower is more liquid; this is the standard academic price-impact measure.   Implied spread — bid-ask spread estimated from daily high-low ranges (Corwin-Schultz), a proxy for round-trip cost.   Order book — actual resting bids and offers, the only genuinely real liquidity here. It's a live 5-level snapshot, so it shifts second to second.
